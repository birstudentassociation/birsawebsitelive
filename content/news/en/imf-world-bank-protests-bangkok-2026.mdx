Farmers, fishing villagers and people from Bangkok's poorest districts marched on the International
Monetary Fund (IMF) and World Bank this week. The 2 institutions are holding their annual meetings
at the Queen Sirikit National Convention Centre from 12 to 18 October 2026.

Police stopped the first march at the Rama IV intersection on 15 October. A second march left
Lumphini Park on 16 October, the day Prime Minister Anutin Charnvirakul opened the meetings. Police
had closed the area around the venue to protest.

The Assembly of the Poor said it did not want to stop the meetings, only to air its grievances.
Those grievances go back 35 years, to the last time the meetings came to Bangkok.

## The banner on the Rama IV footbridge

The 15 October march set off from Khlong Toei at 08:00. At the Rama IV
intersection, police held up signs saying the march was blocking traffic and might break the Public
Assembly Act 2015.

At 09:27, marchers tried to climb the footbridge over the intersection to hang a cloth banner.
Police blocked the stairs. Thai PBS reported that the pushing went on for about 15 minutes. After
talks, the marchers hung the banner from the other side of the bridge. Parts of it had torn.

Police held 12 marchers on the footbridge and released them later, The Reporters said. National
Human Rights Commissioners Wasan Paileeklee and Supatra Nakapew were watching, and helped
negotiate their release.

At least 2 marchers were hurt. A woman said officers pulled her arm and pinned her, injuring her
knee and wrist, and that she heard an officer shout “grab them, grab them.” A man said police
charged in with shields, and that an officer drove a knee into him 3 or 4 times.

A speaker on the protest truck told the officers that “hanging a banner is not a crime” and that
“police must stop using violence.”

Police Lieutenant General Siam Boonsom, who commands the Metropolitan Police, blamed a
miscommunication. He said officers had no order to use force, that police had charged no one, and
that anyone hurt could file a complaint with police or with the commission.

Estimates of the crowd ranged from dozens, by the Associated Press, to about 700, by Thai PBS.
Marchers wore masks of
United States President Donald Trump and IMF Managing Director Kristalina Georgieva, and pulled a
pink pig with dollar signs for eyes.

“Our country is facing a crisis of household debt,” the network said in a statement. “Our people
are growing poorer.” Its representative Lertsak Kamkongsak told the crowd that the 2 lenders had
forced “a state the people built” to live “under the colonialism of loans.”

Supaporn Malailoy, speaking for the network, said the marchers had come from all 4 regions of Thailand.
They had not come to hand in a petition. They wanted to speak directly to the World Bank, the IMF
and the government. About 300 of them slept on Ratchadaphisek Road that night.

about 500. Matichon's morning counts rose from about 400 to 800. They marched 4 abreast in groups wearing green, orange, red and black scarves. The Nation named the communities
behind the banners.

Many of these names are dams, or places a dam changed. Observers from the National Human Rights
Commission and Amnesty International Thailand walked alongside. The Assembly said it would not hand
in a letter to anyone. The Assembly of the Poor marched with La Via
Campesina, a movement of small farmers, which expected members from more than 60 countries.

Paitoon Saloisod is the Assembly's secretary-general. He blames the 2 institutions for steering
Thailand towards cash crops. He says farmers borrowed against their land, lost it, and left for
the cities, and their children stayed behind with grandparents.

## Pak Mun, the river that lost its fish

The Pak Mun Dam sits on the Mun River in Ubon Ratchathani, just before the Mun joins the Mekong. It
began generating power in 1994. The World Bank lent money for it. Villagers say the dam destroyed
the rapids where they fished.

In 2000, the World Commission on Dams studied what had happened. Before the dam, surveys had
recorded 265 fish species in the river basin. The latest survey upstream of the dam found 96. The
commission concluded that at least 50 species that depend on rapids had disappeared.

Families received 90,000 baht each in compensation. Thai PBS reported that 30,000 came as cash and
the other 60,000 had to go into shares in a cooperative. Villagers say the split divided the
community.

“Making a living is hard now,” a villager from Ban Khan Pueai told Thai PBS in 2019. “We can still
eat the small fish. But the fish we sold for money are gone. Our livelihood is gone.”

## 36 years and still waiting

It took 29 years to verify who the dam had harmed. On 12 March 2026, a subcommittee recognised
1,632 families as affected. On 21 September, the main committee, chaired by Energy Minister Ekanat
Promphan, declined to endorse the list and sent it back to the province.

Villagers were allowed in only as silent observers. Buali Phothiwong, who represents them, asked
why “people who are not affected, and are not among the 1,632 families” had the power to decide
for them.

Elders have died waiting. Many of their children and grandchildren have left the river to find
work elsewhere, and families have split, the Assembly of the Poor said.

On 15 October, the Assembly published a statement to the World Bank. It asks the Bank to accept
responsibility for the dam formally, to help fund fair compensation for the 1,632 families, and
to back studies of opening the dam gates for good. It asks the Bank to reply in writing within
30 days.

Tassanai Wangon represents the Pak Mun villagers in the Assembly. Tassanai has one request.

A debt in dollars now took more than twice as many baht to repay. In August, the IMF put together
a 17.2 billion dollar rescue with other lenders. It came with conditions. The government had to

- raise value added tax from 7 to 10 percent
- run a budget surplus of 1 percent of national output
- sell off state enterprises
- close or take over weak lenders

Parliament passed 11 laws in 1998 and 1999 to meet the letter of intent Thailand signed with the
IMF. They changed the rules on bankruptcy, foreclosure and foreign ownership. Critics still call
them the “nation-selling laws.”

On 8 December 1997, the government ordered 56 finance companies to close for good. The IMF's first
plan asked Thailand to run a budget surplus in the middle of the crash. By February 1998 the IMF
had dropped it and allowed a deficit.

## The fire sale

The government set up the Financial Sector Restructuring Authority to sell what the 56 closed
companies owned. Their assets were worth 677,645 million baht on the books. The authority sold
them for 185,226 million.

The country lost about 492,000 million baht, before interest. In 2012 the Criminal Court convicted
the authority's former chair, Amaret Sila-on, and its former secretary-general, Wicharat
Wichitwathakan. They had failed to collect a first payment of 2,304 million baht from Lehman
Brothers Holdings, one of the buyers. Each received a 2-year suspended sentence.

The National Economic and Social Development Board counted 11.4 percent of Thais as poor in 1996.
By 1998 it counted 12.9 percent. The United Nations Development Programme estimated that nearly
1 million people fell back below the poverty line.

In April 1998, the monk Luang Ta Maha Bua began asking Thais to give gold to rebuild the national
reserves. By May 2023 the campaign had handed the Bank of Thailand about 13 tonnes of gold, PPTV
reported.

Thailand paid back the last of its IMF loan on 31 July 2003, ahead of schedule.

## Land that became collateral

The harm began before 1997. In 1982 and 1983, Thailand took 2 World Bank structural adjustment
loans. They came with 10 and then 13 binding conditions, and pushed the economy towards producing
for export.

In 1984, the World Bank gave 183 million dollars to speed up land title deeds for farmers, and the
Land Department ran the titling drive from 1985 to 2004. A title deed made land easy to sell and
easy to borrow against. In the land boom from 1988 to 1991, speculators bought up small farmers'
land, and ownership concentrated, Way magazine reported.

Paitoon Saloisod says the model the World Bank and IMF promoted did the rest. Farmers grew kenaf, cotton and cassava for export, which needed
bought seed and fertiliser. They borrowed ahead of each harvest from middlemen, a practice called
tok khiao. Losses and compound interest piled up until the land was seized or sold.

## One building, 35 years

The Queen Sirikit National Convention Centre opened on 15 October 1991, for the IMF and World Bank
annual meetings. They were the last held in Bangkok before this year.

In the months before, the government forcibly removed more than 2,000 slum residents from around
the site, according to a study later published by Yale Law School. It demolished hundreds of
homes and put up a metal wall to hide the area from delegates.

At the network's press conference on 2 October, Pranom Somwong described the centre as a building
put up for the World Bank “at the cost of evicting the urban poor.”

35 years after the wall went up, the first march to the same building set off from Khlong Toei,
the district around it.

## What they are asking for

La Via Campesina did not ask to meet the IMF or the World Bank. Morgan Ody, a French farmer with the
movement, told reporters on 7 October, “We don't think the World Bank and the IMF can be reformed to serve the interests of
the people.”

The network closed its declaration with one line. “We are engaging in civil disobedience. The
streets are the people's parliament.”

## What happens next

The meetings close on Sunday 18 October. Police restrictions on protest at the 24 sites stay in
place until Tuesday 20 October.

The IMF's latest advice to Thailand, in February 2026, asks the government to “raise revenues to
rebuild buffers” and to commit to a medium-term plan to cut the deficit. It expects public debt to
reach 66.8 percent of national output this year.

The Pak Mun villagers have asked the World Bank to answer them in writing within 30 days.

The People's Network planned to leave Ratchadaphisek Road at 18:00 on 16 October. The Assembly of
the Poor planned to read a statement at 16:30 and end at 17:00. At the time of publishing, no
outlet had reported where the 16 October march stopped. Neither the IMF, the World Bank nor the
government had replied to either group's demands.
