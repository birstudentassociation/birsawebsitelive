Farmers, fishing villagers and people from Bangkok's poorest districts marched on the International
Monetary Fund (IMF) and World Bank this week. The 2 institutions are holding their annual meetings
at the Queen Sirikit National Convention Centre from 12 to 18 October 2026.

Police stopped the first march at the Rama IV intersection on 15 October. A second march left
Lumphini Park on 16 October, the day Prime Minister Anutin Charnvirakul opened the meetings. Police
had closed the area around the venue to protest.

The Assembly of the Poor said it did not want to stop the meetings, only to air its grievances.
Those grievances go back 35 years, to the last time the meetings came to Bangkok.

## The banner on the Rama IV footbridge

The 15 October march set off from the Customs intersection in Khlong Toei at 08:00. At the Rama IV
intersection, police held up signs saying the march was blocking traffic and might break the Public
Assembly Act 2015.

At 09:27, marchers tried to climb the footbridge over the intersection to hang a cloth banner.
Police blocked the stairs. Thai PBS reported that the pushing went on for about 15 minutes. After
talks, the marchers hung the banner from the other side of the bridge. Parts of it had torn.

THE STANDARD reported that some marchers were hurt, without giving a number. Marchers said police
tried to use cable ties on them. The paper carried no police response. A speaker on the protest
truck told the officers that “hanging a banner is not a crime” and that “police must stop using
violence.” No outlet confirmed any arrests.

Staff of the National Human Rights Commission of Thailand watched from the roadside. The marchers
asked them to protect their right to assemble.

Estimates of the crowd ranged from dozens, by the Associated Press, to about 700, by Thai PBS.
Marchers wore masks of
United States President Donald Trump and IMF Managing Director Kristalina Georgieva, and pulled a
pink pig with dollar signs for eyes.

“Our country is facing a crisis of household debt,” the network said in a statement. “Our people
are growing poorer.”

Its spokesperson, Supaporn Malailoy, said the marchers had come from all 4 regions of Thailand.
They had not come to hand in a petition. They wanted to speak directly to the World Bank, the IMF
and the government. About 300 of them slept on Ratchadaphisek Road that night.

## Who walked on 16 October

About 500 people gathered at Gate 1 of Lumphini Park on Friday morning, World Food Day. They marched
4 abreast in groups wearing green, orange, red and black scarves. The Nation named the communities
behind the banners.

Many of these names are dams, or places a dam changed. The Assembly of the Poor marched with La Via
Campesina, a movement of small farmers, which expected members from more than 60 countries.

Paitoon Saloisod is the Assembly's secretary-general. He blames the 2 institutions for steering
Thailand towards cash crops. He says farmers borrowed against their land, lost it, and left for
the cities, and their children stayed behind with grandparents.

## Pak Mun, the river that lost its fish

The Pak Mun Dam sits on the Mun River in Ubon Ratchathani, just before the Mun joins the Mekong. It
began generating power in 1994. The World Bank lent money for it. Villagers say the dam destroyed
the rapids where they fished.

In 2000, the World Commission on Dams studied what had happened. It counted 265 fish species in the
Mun River. It found that the dam had affected 169 of them, and that 56 had disappeared.

## Why 1997 still counts

On 2 July 1997, the Bank of Thailand stopped defending the baht. One United States dollar had cost
about 25 baht. Within months it cost nearly 55.

A debt in dollars now took more than twice as many baht to repay. In August, the IMF put together
a 17.2 billion dollar rescue with other lenders. It came with conditions. The government had to
close or take over weak lenders, keep a tight budget, and change its laws on bankruptcy and foreign
ownership.

On 8 December 1997, the government ordered 56 finance companies to close for good. The IMF's first
plan asked Thailand to run a budget surplus in the middle of the crash. By February 1998 the IMF
had dropped it and allowed a deficit.

The National Economic and Social Development Board counted 11.4 percent of Thais as poor in 1996.
By 1998 it counted 12.9 percent. The United Nations Development Programme estimated that nearly
1 million people fell back below the poverty line.

In April 1998, the monk Luang Ta Maha Bua began asking Thais to give gold to rebuild the national
reserves. The campaign ran for 6 years. His followers count more than 10 tonnes of gold.

Thailand paid back the last of its IMF loan on 31 July 2003, ahead of schedule.

## One building, 35 years

The Queen Sirikit National Convention Centre opened on 15 October 1991, for the IMF and World Bank
annual meetings. They were the last held in Bangkok before this year.

In the months before, the government forcibly removed more than 2,000 slum residents from around
the site, according to a study later published by Yale Law School. It demolished hundreds of
homes and put up a metal wall to hide the area from delegates.

## What they are asking for

La Via Campesina did not ask to meet the IMF or the World Bank. Morgan Ody, a French farmer with the
movement, told reporters
on 7 October, “We don't think the World Bank and the IMF can be reformed to serve the interests of
the people.”

## What happens next

The meetings close on Sunday 18 October. Police restrictions on protest at the 24 sites stay in
place until Tuesday 20 October.

The People's Network planned to leave Ratchadaphisek Road on the evening of 16 October. At the
time of publishing, no outlet had reported whether the 16 October march reached the cordon, or
whether anyone took the demands inside. Neither the IMF, the World Bank nor the government had
replied to them.
