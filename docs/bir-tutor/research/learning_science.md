# Learning-science techniques deliverable through a website (self-study, 17–18 year olds, high-stakes admission exam)

Scope note: numbers below were checked against abstracts or full text in this session where marked "(primary)". Figures taken from secondary summaries are marked "(secondary)" and should be checked before being quoted publicly. "Lab" = laboratory or short experimental study; "field" = real courses, real grades.

## 1. Retrieval practice / testing effect

### Takeaway

Practice testing is the best-supported technique for a self-study site: a medium effect (g ≈ 0.50) in both lab (Rowland 2014) and real classrooms (Yang 2021, 222 studies, ~48,000 students). It is larger with corrective feedback, repeated tests and a match between practice and final-test format; transfer to new question types is real but smaller.

### Cited Findings

- Yang, Luo, Vadillo, Yu & Shanks (2021), Psychological Bulletin 147(4) 399–435: classroom-only meta-analysis, 222 independent studies, 48,478 students. Overall quizzing effect on achievement **g = 0.499** (medium). Moderators include the control-condition learning strategy, test-format consistency, matching of practice and final material, **corrective feedback**, and **number of test repetitions** (primary abstract) — [HKBU record](https://scholars.hkbu.edu.hk/en/publications/testing-quizzing-boosts-classroom-learning-a-systematic-and-meta-/); [PDF](https://gwern.net/docs/spacedrepetition/2021-yang.pdf). A secondary summary gives 573 effect sizes (unverified) — [Tips for Teachers](https://tipsforteachers.substack.com/p/research-bite-4-testing-quizzing).
- Rowland (2014), Psychological Bulletin 140(6) 1432–1463, testing vs restudy (mostly lab): 159 effect sizes from 61 studies, **g = 0.50**; with feedback **g = 0.73**, without **g = 0.39**; initial cued-recall tests beat free recall or recognition (secondary summaries of the paper) — [paper PDF](https://courseware.epfl.ch/assets/courseware/v1/fdde2f0aa590bf3b1324077a6bf1540c/asset-v1%3AEPFL%2BDEMO%2B2020%2Btype%40asset%2Bblock/Rowland2014-meta-analysis.pdf); [secondary numbers](https://studycardsai.com/blog/active-recall-statistics); [moderator summary](https://data.loterre.fr/P66/en/page/-JRJHZ858-D).
- Adesope, Trevisan & Sundararajan (2017), Review of Educational Research 87(3) 659–701: 272 independent effect sizes, ~15,000 participants (article count reported as 118 by most sources, 188 by one); practice tests beat restudy and **all other comparison conditions**; effects amplified for some learners when feedback was given; judged resistant to file-drawer bias (secondary) — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/0034654316689306); [WSU record](https://rex.libraries.wsu.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Rethinking-the-Use-of-Tests-A/99900601154801842); [Learning Scientists summary](https://learningscientists.org/blog/2017/2/9-1?format=amp).
- Dunlosky et al. (2013) rated practice testing one of only two "high utility" techniques (with distributed practice) — [APS release](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade.html).
- Shanks and colleagues have also examined frequent testing and test anxiety (relevant because quizzing does not appear to raise anxiety in classroom data) — [UCL preprint](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10177686/1/Shanks_Frequent%20Tests%20&%20TA-R2.PDF) (not read in full this session).

### Inferences (design implications)

- Make retrieval the default activity, not an optional extra: every lesson ends in a low-stakes quiz, and "review" means re-quizzing, not rereading.
- Always give corrective feedback showing the correct answer and why (Rowland's feedback contrast 0.73 vs 0.39 is the largest lever). Feedback may be delayed slightly (after the item set) without losing the benefit, but must exist.
- Mix formats: use the exam's own format (often MCQ for admission tests) for format consistency, plus short-answer / cued-recall items for initial learning, which produce stronger memory traces in lab work.
- Repeat items across sessions (repetition is a moderator); retire an item only after several correct spaced retrievals.
- For transfer, write practice items that are _new_ applications of the same concept, not only verbatim repeats; expect smaller gains on far-transfer items than on identical items.

### Gaps

- I could not open the Adesope 2017 moderator table this session. From memory (unverified, check before use): overall g ≈ 0.61, MCQ practice ≈ 0.70, short answer ≈ 0.48, mixed formats largest, secondary students larger than postsecondary, feedback not a significant moderator in that dataset. This last point conflicts with Rowland; treat as unresolved.
- The specific Yang 2021 moderator values (e.g. effect with vs without feedback, MCQ vs short answer in classrooms) were not retrieved.

## 2. Spacing, distributed practice and interleaving (4-week and 5-month horizons)

### Takeaway

Spacing has very large lab effects and a well-mapped "optimal gap" curve: the best gap between study sessions is roughly 20–40% of the time-to-test for a one-week horizon falling to 5–10% for a one-year horizon. Interleaving gives a moderate overall effect (g = 0.42) but only a small one for maths (g = 0.34), and it can backfire for word lists.

### Cited Findings

- Cepeda, Pashler, Vul, Wixted & Rohrer (2006), Psychological Bulletin 132(3) 354–380: synthesis of 839 assessments from 317 experiments in 184 articles; the optimal inter-study interval grows as the retention interval grows — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16719566/); [PDF](https://www.evullab.org/pdf/CepedaPashlerVulWixtedRohrer-PB-2006.pdf).
- Cepeda, Vul, Rohrer, Wixted & Pashler (2008), Psychological Science 19(11) 1095–1102 (primary, full text): 1,354 adults (online, mean age 34) learned obscure facts, reviewed after gaps up to 3.5 months, tested up to 1 year later. Optimal gap ≈ **20–40% of a 1-week delay, 5–10% of a 1-year delay**. Fitted optimal gaps for retention intervals of 7, 35, 70 and 350 days were about **1.6, 7, 10 and 25 days** (24%, 19%, 14%, 7%). The optimal gap vs zero gap gave a **64% increase in final recall (d = 1.1)**. Gaps longer than optimal cost much less than gaps shorter than optimal — [PDF](https://www.evullab.org/pdf/CepedaVulRohrerWixtedPashler-PS-2008.pdf); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19076480/).
- Boundary: Cepeda 2008 is factual/verbal recall in adults online, not problem solving or 17-year-olds; it is effectively a large field-like lab study.
- Brunmair & Richter (2019), Psychological Bulletin, doi 10.1037/bul0000209 (primary abstract): 59 studies, 238 effect sizes, 158 samples. Overall **g = 0.42**; paintings g = 0.67; **mathematical tasks g = 0.34**; expository texts non-significant; **words g = −0.39 (blocking better)**. Interleaving helps more when categories are similar to each other, items within a category are dissimilar, and material is complex. Authors advise caution for expository texts and words — [preprint](https://www.psychologie.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06020400/2019/Brunmair_Richter_in_press__2019_META-ANALYSIS_OF_INTERLEAVED_LEARNING.pdf).
- Dunlosky et al. (2013) rated distributed practice "high utility" — [APS release](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade.html).

### Inferences (design implications)

- **4-week horizon** (exam ~28 days away): per Cepeda's ratios (~20–25% at short horizons), re-review each item roughly 1, then ~5–7 days after first learning; front-load new content in weeks 1–2 so every topic gets at least two spaced retrievals before exam day. Erring longer is safer than shorter.
- **5-month horizon** (~150 days): optimal single gap is ~10–15% of the remaining time, i.e. roughly 2–3 weeks between reviews of a topic, tightening as the exam nears (expanding-then-contracting schedule). Build a scheduler that computes review dates from the exam date, not a fixed Leitner interval.
- Use interleaving inside problem-solving practice where the hard part is _choosing_ the method (mixed maths/physics sets, mixed grammar-error types, mixed reading-question types), after an initial blocked introduction. Avoid interleaving vocabulary lists and long expository readings.
- Expect students to _feel_ that spacing and interleaving are worse (desirable difficulty); the site should show their own retention data to counter that.

### Gaps

- No meta-analysis found this session giving spacing effect sizes specifically for 16–18 year olds in real exam prep; classroom spacing trials exist (e.g. Rohrer's maths studies) but were not retrieved.
- The "10–20%" rule of thumb often quoted is a simplification; the paper itself gives 20–40% (1 week) to 5–10% (1 year), and suggests ~15–20% as a rough general ratio with caveats.

## 3. Worked examples, faded examples, cognitive load, expertise reversal, annotated exemplars

### Takeaway

Worked examples reliably help novices (maths meta-analysis g = 0.48), especially correct examples with self-explanation prompts, but the benefit reverses as learners gain expertise, so examples should fade into independent problem solving.

### Cited Findings

- Barbieri, Clerjuste & Chawla (2023), Educational Psychology Review: 43 articles, 55 studies, 181 effect sizes; worked examples on maths performance **g = 0.48** (robust variance estimation). Correct-only examples beat incorrect-only or mixed; adding **self-explanation prompts** rescued studies that otherwise showed negative effects — [Kent State record](https://oaks.kent.edu/hcri/meta-analysis-worked-examples-effect-mathematics-performance); [poster](https://www.cehd.udel.edu/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/Worked-Examples-Meta-Poster_022722_SC.pdf). Press summary: ~18 percentile points — [UDel](https://www.cehd.udel.edu/in-the-news-christina-barbieri/).
- Expertise reversal: techniques that help novices "can lose their effectiveness and even have negative consequences" with more experienced learners (Kalyuga 2007, Educational Psychology Review) — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007%2Fs10648-007-9054-3); [UOW](https://ro.uow.edu.au/articles/journal_contribution/The_expertise_reversal_effect/27724950/1).
- A 2025 meta-analysis of the expertise reversal effect exists (Tetzlaff, Simonsmeier, Peters & Brod, Learning and Instruction 98, 102142, doi 10.1016/j.learninstruc.2025.102142); its numbers were not retrieved — [concept entry](https://loterre.istex.fr/P66/en/page/-R5C8JXLW-X).
- Faded worked examples are the standard remedy for expertise reversal, structuring the move from studying examples to solving problems — [Wikipedia overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worked-example_effect) (secondary).
- Expertise reversal has been shown even for explanatory notes on Shakespeare texts, i.e. beyond maths — [UOW paper](https://ro.uow.edu.au/edupapers/140).

### Inferences (design implications)

- For each problem type: full worked example → example with one step blanked → two steps blanked → independent problem; advance a student based on accuracy, and let strong students skip straight to problems (pre-test or "try first" gate).
- Attach a self-explanation prompt to every example ("Why is step 3 allowed?").
- For essays/writing: annotated model answers (examples labelled with the marking criteria they meet) are the writing analogue of worked examples; follow with partial-completion tasks (e.g. write only the conclusion), then full essays. Evidence for this transfer to essays is by analogy, not from the maths meta-analysis.
- Keep pages low in extraneous load: worked steps physically next to the diagram, no decorative media.

### Gaps

- No meta-analytic effect size found for annotated model essays / exemplars specifically.
- Tetzlaff et al. 2025 effect sizes and whether fading was a moderator in Barbieri 2023 were not verified.

## 4. Feedback: what works and what backfires

### Takeaway

Feedback averages a medium effect (d = 0.48), far below the old Visible Learning figure of ~0.7–0.79, and in over a third of comparisons feedback made performance worse. Task- and process-level information helps most; praise or person-focused feedback tends not to.

### Cited Findings

- Wisniewski, Zierer & Hattie (2020), Frontiers in Psychology (primary abstract): 435 studies, k = 994, N > 61,000; random-effects **d = 0.48**; strong heterogeneity; impact depends on information content; larger effects on cognitive and motor outcomes than on motivational/behavioural ones. Earlier Visible Learning syntheses claimed 0.70–0.79 — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03087/full); [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6987456).
- Kluger & DeNisi (1996), Psychological Bulletin 119, 254–284: 131 studies, 607 effect sizes, 23,663 observations; mean **d = 0.41**, but feedback **reduced performance in over one third of cases**, not explained by sampling error or feedback sign. Their account: feedback that pulls attention toward the self (rather than the task) lowers performance — [abstract](https://pluto.huji.ac.il/%7Emskluger/files/Download/Kluger%20&%20%20DeNisi%20abstract.doc); [full text](https://mrbartonmaths.com/resourcesnew/8.%20Research/Marking%20and%20Feedback/The%20effects%20of%20feedback%20interventions.pdf).
- Hattie & Timperley (2007), Review of Educational Research 77(1), doi 10.3102/003465430298487: model of four feedback levels (task, process, self-regulation, self); self-level feedback (praise) is the least effective (not re-read this session) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3102/003465430298487).
- EEF toolkit lists feedback around +6 months (older versions +8) and warns it can sometimes make things worse — [Research School](https://researchschool.org.uk/huntington/news/unpacking-the-eef-toolkit); [Tutopiya](https://www.tutopiya.com/tools/for-schools/school-blog/formative-assessment-strategies-igcse-classrooms-tools-save-time/) (secondary).
- In testing research, corrective feedback raised the testing effect from g ≈ 0.39 to 0.73 (Rowland 2014, secondary) — see section 1.

### Inferences (design implications)

- Feedback on every answer should say what is right, why, and what to do next (task + process); avoid "You're a natural!" or ranking against other students.
- Avoid leaderboards and normative comparisons as primary feedback; show progress against the student's own past performance and against the exam standard.
- Show the reasoning for wrong MCQ options (common misconception), since information content drives the effect.
- Track the third-of-cases risk: A/B test feedback designs on the site rather than assuming any feedback helps.

### Gaps

- No good evidence found this session on optimal feedback timing (immediate vs delayed) for online exam prep specifically.

## 5. Mastery learning, adaptive practice and intelligent tutoring systems

### Takeaway

Mastery learning shows moderate gains (≈0.5 SD) mainly on teacher-made tests; ITSs give ≈0.4 SD over classroom teaching and ≈0.66 SD median in rigorous evaluations, roughly matching human tutoring. Bloom's "2 sigma" has not been reproduced; human tutoring averages ≈0.79.

### Cited Findings

- Kulik, Kulik & Bangert-Drowns (1990), Review of Educational Research: 108 controlled evaluations; average **ES ≈ 0.52** on exam performance; strongest for weaker students; **self-paced mastery programmes often reduced completion rates** in college classes (secondary summaries) — [Great Hearts summary](https://institute.greatheartsamerica.org/media/scholarship-and-research/research-on-mastery-learning-including-a-comparison-of-the-bloom-model-and-the-fwtm-model/); [academia.edu](https://www.academia.edu/81783373/Effectiveness_of_Mastery_Learning_Programs_A_Meta_Analysis).
- Slavin (1987) found essentially no effect of group-based mastery learning on **standardised** measures, though experimenter-made tests showed gains — [PDF](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/1987-slavin.pdf).
- Bloom's two-sigma claim, and critiques — [Nintil review](https://nintil.com/bloom-sigma/) (secondary).
- VanLehn (2011), Educational Psychologist 46(4) 197–221: answer-based tutoring **0.31** (165 effects), step-based **0.76** (28), substep-based **0.40** (26), human tutoring **0.79** (10). Step-based systems roughly match human tutors; finer granularity does not keep adding benefit — [VanLehn slides](https://quality.mit.edu/files/2012/01/Quality-Symposium-Kurt-VanLehn.pdf); [Penn course page](https://learninganalytics.upenn.edu/ryanbaker/ITS2020/topical-wk-1/its-8.html).
- Ma, Adesope, Nesbit & Liu (2014), Journal of Educational Psychology 106(4) 901–918 (primary abstract): 107 effect sizes, 14,321 participants. ITS vs teacher-led large-group **g = 0.42**; vs non-ITS computer instruction **g = 0.57**; vs textbooks/workbooks **g = 0.35**; vs individual human tutoring **g = −0.11** (n.s.); vs small group g = 0.05 (n.s.). Positive at all education levels and as homework aid — [PDF](https://www.cs.uky.edu/~sgware/reading/papers/ma2014intelligent.pdf).
- Kulik & Fletcher (2016), Review of Educational Research: 50 controlled evaluations, **median 0.66 SD** (50th → 75th percentile); much larger on locally developed tests than standardised tests; evaluations failing inclusion criteria showed small effects — [IDA](https://www.ida.org/research-and-publications/publications/all/e/ef/effectiveness-of-intelligent-tutoring-systems-a-meta-analytic-review); [DTIC PDF](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/AD1030353.pdf).
- EEF lists mastery learning ≈ +5 months with inconsistent results (older figure, secondary) — [Teachwire](https://www.teachwire.net/?p=10332).

### Inferences (design implications)

- Give step-level feedback (check each line of a worked solution, or hint at the next step) rather than only right/wrong on final answers; VanLehn's contrast (0.76 vs 0.31) is the strongest argument for the extra build cost.
- Use mastery gates (e.g. 80–90% on a fresh item set before unlocking) but with a time budget: the Kulik finding that self-paced mastery lowers completion is a direct warning for a self-paced site with a fixed exam date. Allow "move on and come back" with spaced re-checks rather than hard locks.
- Expect gains on our own quizzes to overstate gains on the real exam (Kulik & Fletcher, Slavin); validate with full past-paper mocks.
- Do not market "2 sigma" outcomes; a realistic target is 0.3–0.6 SD.

### Gaps

- Most ITS evidence is maths/science; little found on reading, essay or language ITSs for admission exams.

## 6. Self-regulation, metacognition, planning, mindset, values affirmation, test anxiety

### Takeaway

Metacognition/self-regulation teaching scores well in the EEF toolkit (+7 months) and implementation intentions have a medium-large effect on goal attainment (d ≈ 0.65), but single-session "psychological" interventions show small or null effects at scale: growth mindset effects are weak overall and limited to lower achievers; values affirmation has failed large replications; test-anxiety writing evidence is small and mixed.

### Cited Findings

- EEF: metacognition and self-regulation **+7 months** (lowered from +8 after new studies) — [EEF blog](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/news/latest-updates-to-the-toolkit/).
- Implementation intentions (Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006): **d = 0.65** (95% CI ≈ 0.6–0.7), 94 independent tests, >8,000 participants (secondary) — [Frontiers citing it](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.565202/pdf); [summary](https://www.thebehavioralscientist.com/glossary/implementation-intentions).
- Sisk et al. (2018), Psychological Science 29(4) 549–571 (primary abstract): mindset–achievement correlation meta-analysis k = 273, N = 365,915; intervention meta-analysis k = 43, N = 57,155; "**overall effects were weak** for both"; low-SES or academically at-risk students might benefit. Correlation reported as r ≈ 0.10 in summaries — [PDF](<https://englelab.gatech.edu/articles/2018/Sisk,%20Burgoyne%20et%20al.%20(2018)%20-%20Mindset%20and%20Academic%20Achievement.pdf>); [MSU release](https://msutoday.msu.edu/news/2018/05/study-finds-popular-growth-mindset-educational-interventions-arent-very-effective).
- Yeager et al. (2019), Nature 573 364–369, National Study of Learning Mindsets: <1 hour online intervention, nationally representative US 9th-graders (~6,700 treated, ~6,720 control), preregistered with independent data processing and blinded Bayesian analysis. Improved grades **among lower-achieving students** and increased advanced-maths enrolment overall; effects depended on peer norms aligning with the message — [HKS](https://www.hks.harvard.edu/publications/national-experiment-reveals-where-growth-mindset-improves-achievement); [UT PRC brief](https://liberalarts.utexas.edu/prc/our-research/prc-brief-series/education-work-and-institutions/change-students-mindsets-to-improve-academic-achievement.html). A secondary source puts the lower-achiever gain at **+0.10 GPA points** (unverified) — [summary](https://www.youngju.dev/transcribe/psych-papers/growth-mindset-evidence.en).
- Yeager et al. (2022), Nature 607 512–520, "synergistic mindsets" (growth mindset + stress-is-enhancing), ~30 min online, six double-blind RCTs, N = 4,291, lab and field; improved stress responses and anxiety, and in one study course pass rates a year later (press sources) — [NICHD](https://www.nichd.nih.gov/newsroom/news/070622-teen-stress); [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nat/nature/v607y2022i7919d10.1038_s41586-022-04907-7.html).
- Stress reappraisal (Jamieson et al. 2010): students told arousal aids performance scored higher on a practice GRE-maths section and on their **actual GRE** 1–3 months later (small lab sample); 2022 field study with 93 community-college maths students linked reappraisal to lower anxiety and higher exam scores — [Harvard DASH](https://dash.harvard.edu/handle/1/4214916); [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/reconceptualizing-stress-as-a-coping-tool-improves-students-performance-on-upcoming-math-exam/).
- Expressive writing (Ramirez & Beilock 2011, Science): highly test-anxious 9th-grade biology students who wrote about worries before a final outperformed controls; lab component used ~20 students — [UChicago](https://news.uchicago.edu/story/writing-about-worries-eases-anxiety-and-improves-test-performance); [Science News](https://www.sciencenews.org/article/write-stuff-test-anxiety). Later studies are mixed: a 110-student thesis found no effect on performance or anxiety [Syracuse](https://surface.syr.edu/etd/373); an Ohio State study's effect vanished after controlling for prior grades [OSU](https://kb.osu.edu/items/53c1ec82-926e-5168-a697-02a1bd93c3f0); a graduate-entrance-exam study found benefits only for MCAT/LSAT takers [Fordham](https://research.library.fordham.edu/psych_facultypubs/197). No large preregistered replication was found.
- Values affirmation: Hanselman et al. (2017), a well-powered replication in a setting where the original field effects had been found, found **no effect** and ruled out effects larger than 0.10 — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5403146); Serra-Garcia, Hansen & Gneezy (2020) reanalysis of Miyake et al. 2010 questioned the original result — [preprint](https://rady.ucsd.edu/_files/faculty-research/uri-gneezy/short%20psychological.pdf); [FORRT](https://forrt.org/flora-replication-atlas/doi/10.1177/0956797620923587/).

### Inferences (design implications)

- Build in planning features with proven mechanics: weekly study plan, "if it is 7pm on Tuesday, then I do 20 minutes of reading questions" prompts (implementation intentions), and calibration prompts ("How confident are you?") before revealing answers, then show the confidence-vs-accuracy gap.
- A short reappraisal / synergistic-mindset module before mock exams is cheap and has the best recent evidence of the anxiety interventions; frame it as optional and do not promise score gains.
- Do not invest in values-affirmation exercises; treat expressive writing as an optional pre-exam tool with weak evidence.
- Growth-mindset messaging is unlikely to move average scores; any effect is concentrated in lower achievers and depends on context.

### Gaps

- No meta-analytic effect size found for calibration/judgement-of-learning prompts in online exam prep.
- Sisk 2018 intervention effect size (often cited as d ≈ 0.08) and Yeager 2019 effect sizes not verified from primary text.

## 7. Online self-paced learning: drop-off and nudges

### Takeaway

Self-paced online courses lose most learners (MOOC completion typically under 10%); intention to complete is a strong predictor. Light-touch behavioural nudges tested across ~250,000 learners produced effects about ten times smaller than in pilots and did not raise completion.

### Cited Findings

- Kizilcec et al. (2020), PNAS 117 14900–14905: interventions tested in 247 Harvard/MIT/Stanford courses with ~250,000 learners; **self-regulation (planning) prompts raised early engagement but not completion**; value-relevance writing helped only learners in developing countries in courses with a global achievement gap; machine-learning targeting added little — [Imperial record](https://spiral.imperial.ac.uk/entities/publication/ab760912-87ee-48e0-97c8-c278a787107a); [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7334459).
- Effects shrank by about a factor of ten moving from a few courses to hundreds — [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2020/06/200615152116.htm).
- Reich & Ruipérez-Valiente (2019), Science 363(6423), "The MOOC pivot": completion typically under 10% with no improvement over years (as cited by others) — [MIT DSpace](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/136215.2?show=full); [citing paper](https://www.redalyc.org/journal/3314/331479376022/331479376022.pdf).
- Among those who intended to complete, completion was 19.5% vs 5.4% for others (earlier Harvard/MIT data) — [CACM](https://cacmb4.acm.org/news/181086-rethinking-low-completion-rates-in-moocs).
- Wang & Baker (2018): grit and goal orientation predict completion independently of intention — [IRRODL](https://www.erudit.org/fr/revues/irrodl/2018-v19-n3-irrodl03963/1051256ar.pdf).
- Self-paced mastery programmes reduced completion rates in college (Kulik et al. 1990, secondary) — see section 5.

### Inferences (design implications)

- Our users differ from MOOC learners (a fixed high-stakes exam date is a strong external deadline), so drop-off should be lower, but do not rely on reminders or one-off nudges to fix engagement.
- Use the exam date to drive a concrete schedule with daily targets and visible deadlines; record intended study commitment at sign-up as an early-risk signal.
- Run proper A/B tests of nudges at our scale; assume pilot-sized effects will shrink.

### Gaps

- Evidence on SMS/email reminder effects specifically for secondary-age self-study was not retrieved this session.

## 8. Dunlosky et al. 2013 utility ratings

### Takeaway

Of ten common techniques, only practice testing and distributed practice were rated high utility; highlighting, rereading and summarisation (among the most used by students) were rated low.

### Cited Findings

- Dunlosky, Rawson, Marsh, Nathan & Willingham (2013), Psychological Science in the Public Interest 14(1), doi 10.1177/1529100612453266: high utility = **practice testing, distributed practice**; five techniques rated low, including **summarisation, highlighting/underlining and rereading**. Lead author: replacing rereading with delayed retrieval would help students — [APS release](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade.html); [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2013/01/130110111734.htm).
- Moderate utility in the monograph: elaborative interrogation, self-explanation, interleaved practice; low: keyword mnemonic and imagery for text in addition to the three above (from the monograph; not re-verified this session) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266).

### Inferences (design implications)

- Do not build highlighting or "reread notes" features as study modes; any notes view should end with a retrieval check.
- Self-explanation prompts (moderate utility) pair naturally with worked examples (section 3).

### Gaps

- Dunlosky's ratings are expert judgements from the literature to 2012, not a meta-analysis; they include no pooled effect sizes.
